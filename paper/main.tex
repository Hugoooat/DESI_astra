\title{Environment-dependent galaxy clustering from ASTRA tessellations:\\
a comparison with density-split clustering}
\author{Hugo Nguyen}
\date{}

\begin{document}

\maketitle

\begin{abstract}
Density-split clustering, the clustering of regions ranked by their smoothed local density, recovers part of the non-Gaussian cosmological information of the galaxy field. We compare it with ASTRA split, in which the environment of a point is defined without smoothing, from its neighbours in the Delaunay tessellation of the galaxies and of a random catalogue. Both are measured on the same DESI-like luminous red galaxy mocks at $z=0.5$ (AbacusSummit), with the same random points and estimators. Because the galaxy--halo connection (HOD) is drawn independently in each cosmology of the emulator suite, finite differences between cosmologies are dominated by the HOD change; we obtain the cosmological derivatives from a neural-network emulator of the statistics as a function of cosmology and HOD, cross-checked with a regression over the derivative grid, and the covariance from 1786 independent boxes of the official DESI mocks. A second analysis with Quijote halos provides clean finite-difference derivatives, and an MCMC inference with the emulator tests the full pipeline. Both methods improve on the two-point function by factors 1.7--2.7. They carry comparable information: the ratio of their marginalised errors on $\omega_b$, $\omega_{\rm cdm}$, $n_s$ and $\sigma_8$ lies between 0.75 and 1.1, density split being slightly ahead on most parameters. They are complementary: their combination improves on the better of the two by 25\% ($\sigma_8$) to a factor 2.4 ($\omega_b$). With Quijote halos, ASTRA split is more constraining on $\sigma_8$ by a factor 1.5--2 when its environment auto-correlations or tracer environments are used. ASTRA is an adaptive-scale statistic, much more sensitive than density split to the sampling density. We document the methodological pitfalls met along the way (HOD mismatch of emulator suites, single-box covariances, noisy derivatives of environment statistics), how we addressed them, and how the results evolved.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
% =====================================================================

The large-scale distribution of galaxies is one of the most powerful probes of modern cosmology. Galaxy clustering constrains the expansion history, the growth of structure, the matter content and the properties of dark energy and neutrinos. Spectroscopic surveys such as DESI now map galaxies in volumes where the statistical errors are small enough that the information beyond the two-point function becomes worth extracting.

In most analyses, cosmological information is compressed into two-point statistics, the correlation function or the power spectrum. These are optimal for Gaussian fields and remain extremely successful on large scales. The late-time matter distribution, however, becomes strongly non-Gaussian through non-linear gravitational evolution, halo formation and redshift-space distortions (RSD), and part of the information is not captured by two-point summaries. This has motivated many complementary statistics: higher-order correlation functions, marked statistics, counts-in-cells, void statistics, and methods based on a segmentation of the cosmic web. By conditioning clustering measurements on environment, they access the local geometry of structure formation and can break degeneracies of standard analyses.

Among them, density-split clustering \cite{Paillas2023, Paillas2024} ranks random points by the smoothed galaxy density around them, splits them into quantiles, and measures the auto-correlation of each quantile and its cross-correlation with the galaxies. Combined with a simulation-based emulator \cite{CuestaLazaro2024}, it improves $\Lambda$CDM constraints by factors of several with respect to the two-point function.

ASTRA \cite{ASTRA} classifies the cosmic web from the Delaunay tessellation of the galaxies together with a uniform random catalogue: the environment of a point is set by the fraction of galaxies among its neighbours, i.e.\ at an adaptive, local scale and without smoothing. We ask whether environment quartiles defined with ASTRA carry cosmological information beyond the galaxy two-point function, and how they compare with density split when both are measured on the same mocks, with the same random points and the same estimators.

We use three sets of simulations, each for what the others cannot provide (Section~\ref{sec:sims}): the AbacusSummit emulator suite of DESI-like mocks, on which the measurements and the emulator are built; the official DESI AbacusHOD mocks, which provide an independent covariance; and the Quijote simulations, which provide clean finite-difference derivatives. Estimating derivatives and covariances turned out to be the main difficulty of the analysis. Section~\ref{sec:env} presents the two environment estimators, Section~\ref{sec:clustering} the clustering measurements, Section~\ref{sec:framework} the covariance, the derivatives (finite differences, emulator, regression) and the Fisher formalism, Section~\ref{sec:choices} the successive analysis choices and how the results evolved, Section~\ref{sec:results} the results (DESI-like mocks, Quijote, inference), Section~\ref{sec:robustness} the robustness tests, and Section~\ref{sec:discussion} the discussion. Appendix~\ref{app:repro} explains how to reproduce the paper, Appendix~\ref{app:maths} gives the mathematical details, and Appendix~\ref{app:path} details the methodological path.

\subsection{AbacusSummit emulator suite}

We use the HOD galaxy catalogues built for the DESI emulator programme on top of the AbacusSummit $N$-body suite \cite{Maksimova2021}. Each simulation evolves $6912^3$ particles in a periodic box of $2\,h^{-1}{\rm Gpc}$; we use the $z=0.5$ snapshot. Halos are populated with luminous red galaxies (LRG) with the AbacusHOD model \cite{Yuan2022}, whose 12 parameters (central and satellite occupation, velocity bias, environment-based assembly bias) are sampled in the prior of \cite{Yuan2023}. Every catalogue contains $4\times10^6$ galaxies, $\bar n = 5\times10^{-4}\,h^3{\rm Mpc}^{-3}$. The suite covers 85 cosmologies.

The catalogues are stored as \texttt{c\{cosmo\}\_ph000/seed0/hod\{NNN\}.fits}. Two properties of this suite drive our methodology:
\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{One phase per cosmology.} All cosmologies share the initial conditions \texttt{ph000}, with a single HOD seed.
\item \textbf{One HOD draw per file.} The 500 files \texttt{hod000}--\texttt{hod499} of a cosmology have \emph{different} HOD parameters (a Latin hypercube for emulator training), and the parameters behind a given index differ from one cosmology to another.
\end{itemize}
Different HOD files therefore cannot be used as independent realisations of the same model: the scatter between HOD draws reaches $\sim55\%$ of the amplitude of the density-split monopoles, against $\sim1\%$ for the cosmological response we want to measure.

\paragraph{Cosmologies and parameters.} Besides the fiducial Planck 2018 $\Lambda$CDM cosmology (\texttt{c000}), we use the AbacusSummit linear derivative grid \texttt{c100}--\texttt{c113}: seven pairs, each varying one parameter ($\omega_b$, $\omega_{\rm cdm}$, $n_s$, $\alpha_s$, $w_0$, $w_0$ and $w_a$ together, $\sigma_8$) around the fiducial, with $h$ and $A_s$ adjusted to keep the angular size of the sound horizon $\theta_*$ and, for \texttt{c100}--\texttt{c105}, $\sigma_8$ fixed. Derivatives with respect to $\omega_b$, $\omega_{\rm cdm}$ and $n_s$ are therefore taken at fixed $\sigma_8$. All seven pairs are used to train and validate the emulator; the Fisher forecasts are restricted to the four $\Lambda$CDM parameters of Table~\ref{tab:cosmo}, the parameters common with the density-split forecasts of \cite{Paillas2023}, the other cosmological parameters ($\alpha_s$, $N_{\rm ur}$, $w_0$, $w_a$) being fixed to their fiducial values in the Fisher matrices and free in the MCMC inference (Section~\ref{sec:mcmc}).

\paragraph{Positions.} The line of sight is one axis of the box ($z$ by default, $x$ and $y$ for the subvolume covariance). Redshift-space positions are \texttt{X\_PERP}, \texttt{Y\_PERP}, \texttt{Z\_RSD}; real-space positions use \texttt{Z\_PERP}. These columns already include the Alcock--Paczynski rescaling of the fiducial cosmology ($q_\perp$, $q_\parallel$, stored in the headers), so the box is $2000/q_\perp$ across and $2000/q_\parallel$ along the line of sight.

\subsection{Official DESI AbacusHOD LRG mocks}
\label{sec:official}

The emulator suite contains a single phase per cosmology, so its only source of covariance is the 64 subvolumes of one box, measured along three correlated lines of sight (192 samples). This limits the length of the data vectors (Hartlap factor) and, as we found, biases the comparison of the statistics (Section~\ref{sec:choices}). We therefore also use the official DESI AbacusHOD LRG mocks at $z=0.5$ \cite{Yuan2022} (\texttt{AbacusHOD\_mocks/v1}), generated with one best-fit HOD (model \texttt{velbias}, fitted to early DESI LRG clustering):
\begin{itemize}
\item the 1786 AbacusSummit \emph{small} boxes (\texttt{c000}, phases 3000+), of side $500\Mpch$ and independent initial conditions;
\item the 25 \emph{base} boxes of \texttt{c000} (phases 0--24), of side $2\,h^{-1}$Gpc.
\end{itemize}
Choices. (i) Each small box is treated exactly like one subvolume of the main analysis: periodic images for the tessellation, density field smoothed on the periodic box, and the \emph{open} Landy--Szalay estimator with geometry randoms for the clustering, so that the covariance corresponds to the same measurement. A periodic small box has no modes larger than its side, whereas a subvolume of a larger box is affected by them (super-sample covariance); the base boxes (64 subvolumes $\times$ 25 phases) allow this effect to be measured. (ii) Positions are used as provided, the $z$ coordinate including RSD; only the $z$ line of sight is available. (iii) The best-fit HOD lies inside the prior of the emulator suite and its number density ($62\,900$ galaxies per small box) matches that of our catalogues ($62\,500$ per subvolume), which matters for ASTRA (Section~\ref{sec:robustness}). We therefore take it as the fiducial point of the baseline forecasts and of the inference, with this covariance. These mocks contain a single cosmology: they do not provide derivatives.

\subsection{Quijote simulations}
\label{sec:quijote_sims}

The derivatives cannot be estimated cleanly by finite differences in the emulator suite, because the HOD differs between cosmologies. The Quijote simulations \cite{Quijote}, used by \cite{Paillas2023} for density-split Fisher forecasts, provide what is missing: for each parameter, 500 pairs of simulations with $\pm$ variations of $\Omega_m$ ($\pm0.01$), $\Omega_b$ ($\pm0.002$), $h$ ($\pm0.02$), $n_s$ ($\pm0.02$), $\sigma_8$ ($\pm0.015$), and simulations with $M_\nu = 0.1, 0.2, 0.4$\,eV, all sharing the initial seeds of the corresponding fiducial realisations, plus thousands of fiducial realisations for the covariance. Each is a periodic box of $1\,h^{-1}$Gpc with $512^3$ particles.

Choices. (i) Tracers: all Rockstar host halos at $z=0.5$ (no subhalos, at least 100 particles). Their number density, $n\simeq5\times10^{-5}\,h^3{\rm Mpc}^{-3}$, is ten times lower than that of the LRG mocks and cannot be raised with these catalogues. Since ASTRA is an adaptive-scale statistic (Section~\ref{sec:robustness}), its effective scale, the distance between neighbours $\propto\bar n^{-1/3}$, is larger in Quijote ($\simeq27\Mpch$) than for the LRGs ($\simeq13\Mpch$). Both methods are measured on the same halos, so the comparison of ASTRA and density split remains like-for-like, but at another density: absolute errors and ratios should not be compared directly between the Quijote and AbacusSummit analyses. (ii) Redshift space along $z$: $z_s = z + v_z/[aH(a)]$, with the $\Omega_m$ of each simulation. (iii) The whole box is one volume, with periodic images for the tessellation and the natural estimator of a periodic box (analytic random pairs) for the clustering; density split uses the same smoothing ($R_s=10\Mpch$) as for the LRGs. (iv) Derivatives are paired central differences averaged over the realisations shared by the $+$ and $-$ sets; for $M_\nu$, the three-point forward difference $(-3f_0+4f_{0.1}-f_{0.2})/0.2$ with the Zel'dovich-initialised fiducial set (\texttt{fiducial\_ZA}) for $M_\nu=0$, as is standard for Quijote. Because the tracers are the same halos in all simulations, these derivatives have no HOD mismatch. The price is realism: halos are not DESI LRGs, so Quijote serves as a controlled comparison of the information content of the two methods, complementary to the DESI-like analysis.

\section{Environment estimators}
\label{sec:env}
% =====================================================================

Both estimators are evaluated on the \emph{full periodic box}, and on the same set of $N_r = N_g$ uniform random points (fixed seed, drawn in the unit cube and rescaled to the box, so that the random points are identical in all cosmologies up to the AP rescaling).

\subsection{ASTRA split}

The galaxies and random points are joined into a single catalogue and Delaunay-triangulated. For every point (galaxy or random), with $N_{\rm data}$ and $N_{\rm rand}$ the numbers of galaxies and randoms among its Delaunay neighbours,
\begin{equation}
r = \frac{N_{\rm data}-N_{\rm rand}}{N_{\rm data}+N_{\rm rand}} \in [-1,1].
\label{eq:astra_r}
\end{equation}
A point surrounded by galaxies ($r\to1$) lies in a dense region, a point surrounded by randoms ($r\to-1$) in an underdense one. In the original ASTRA classification \cite{ASTRA}, thresholds on $r$ define voids, sheets, filaments and knots; here we use $r$ as a continuous environment variable. The triangulation is computed independently in each subvolume (Section~\ref{sec:clustering}) on a region padded by $30\Mpch$ on each side, with periodic wrapping across the faces of the box, and $r$ is kept only for the points inside the subvolume. This removes the edge effects of a non-periodic triangulation (missing neighbours, elongated boundary simplices). The mean inter-point separation of the joint catalogue is $\simeq10\Mpch$, so the padding contains several layers of neighbours (convergence in Section~\ref{sec:robustness}).

\subsection{Density split}

As a reference we use the standard density split \cite{Paillas2023}. The galaxies are assigned to a periodic mesh of $5\Mpch$ cells with a cloud-in-cell (CIC) scheme, the overdensity field is smoothed with a Gaussian kernel of radius $R_s=10\Mpch$ by FFT, and the smoothed density contrast $\delta_R$ is read (CIC) at the position of every galaxy and random point.

\subsection{Environment quartiles}

For each method, each subvolume and each population, points are ranked by their environment value ($r$ or $\delta_R$) and split into four quartiles of exactly equal size, Q1 (most underdense) to Q4 (most overdense). Because $r$ is a ratio of small integers, many points share the same value; ties are broken at random. We consider two populations, ranked separately so that the thresholds of one do not depend on the other:
\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{random quartiles}: the random points ranked by their environment, the query points of density split, sampling environments by volume;
\item \textbf{galaxy quartiles}: the galaxies ranked by their own environment, sampling environments by mass.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Comparison of the two classifications}

Figure~\ref{fig:env} compares the two estimators on the fiducial catalogue. They are strongly but not perfectly correlated: the Spearman rank correlation between $r$ and $\delta_R$ is $0.719\pm0.005$ for random points and $0.713\pm0.005$ for galaxies (scatter over subvolumes). The extreme quartiles are the most consistent: 63\% of the ASTRA Q1 (Q4) random points are also in the density-split Q1 (Q4), whereas the intermediate quartiles are largely mixed, and less than 2\% of the points move between the two extremes. ASTRA identifies the same large-scale structures as density split but redistributes many points (Figure~\ref{fig:map}), as expected from an adaptive rather than a fixed scale. The distributions of $r$ and $\delta_R$ per quartile are shown in Figure~\ref{fig:envhist}.

\paragraph{Response to cosmology.} Figure~\ref{fig:response} shows how the mean environment of each quartile of the Quijote halos shifts when a cosmological parameter is varied, in units of its scatter between realisations. The density-split contrast $\delta_R$ responds strongly (up to $7\sigma$ for $\sigma_8$), antisymmetrically for the $\pm$ variations; the ASTRA value $r$ hardly moves ($<1\sigma$). This is expected: $r$ is normalised locally by the random points and is nearly insensitive to the overall amplitude of the field. The cosmological information of ASTRA split therefore lies in the clustering of its environments rather than in the values of $r$.

\section{Clustering measurements}
\label{sec:clustering}
% =====================================================================

\subsection{Subvolumes and estimator}

Each $2\,h^{-1}$Gpc box is divided into $4^3=64$ non-overlapping cubic subvolumes of $500\Mpch$ (about $62\,500$ galaxies each). Each subvolume is treated as an independent survey with open boundaries: correlation functions are measured with the Landy--Szalay estimator \cite{LandySzalay1993},
\begin{equation}
\xi(s,\mu) = \frac{D_1D_2 - D_1R - RD_2 + RR}{RR},
\end{equation}
using a uniform catalogue of $5\times$ the number of galaxies to describe the geometry (the same for all subvolumes, so that $RR$ is computed once). Pair counts are computed with \texttt{pycorr}/\texttt{Corrfunc} \cite{Sinha2020} in 30 bins of $5\Mpch$ in $s\in[0,150]\Mpch$ and 120 bins in $\mu\in[-1,1]$, with $\mu$ defined with respect to the fixed line of sight, and projected on the Legendre multipoles $\ell=0,2,4$ (only $\ell=0,2$ are used in the forecasts).

\subsection{Statistics}

For each method $m\in\{$ASTRA, DS$\}$, population $p\in\{$random, galaxy$\}$ and quartile Q$_i$ we measure the quartile--galaxy cross-correlation $\xi^{\rm QG}_{i,\ell}(s)$, between the points of Q$_i$ and all galaxies, and the quartile auto-correlation $\xi^{\rm QQ}_{i,\ell}(s)$, together with the galaxy two-point correlation function $\xi^{\rm GG}_\ell(s)$ (2PCF): 33 statistics per catalogue. For random quartiles, $\xi^{\rm QG}$ and $\xi^{\rm QQ}$ are the analogues of the density-split cross- and auto-correlation functions \cite{Paillas2023}. For galaxy quartiles, which partition the galaxies into four equal sets, the average of the four quartile--galaxy cross-correlations is the 2PCF: the quartiles decompose the 2PCF by environment.

\subsection{Results on the fiducial cosmology}

Figure~\ref{fig:multipoles} compares the extreme quartiles of the two methods, and Figures~\ref{fig:mp_astra} and~\ref{fig:mp_ds} show all four quartiles of each method in the layout of the density-split analyses \cite{Paillas2023}; the galaxy quartiles are shown in Figure~\ref{fig:multipoles_gal}.

The two methods produce qualitatively identical signals. The Q1--galaxy cross-correlation is negative at small separations, the profile of galaxies around underdense regions, and the Q4--galaxy cross-correlation is positive and above the galaxy 2PCF, the profile around overdense regions. The auto-correlations of the extreme quartiles are strongly enhanced with respect to the 2PCF. The quadrupoles have opposite signs for Q1 and Q4: around underdense regions, peculiar velocities point outwards and stretch the void profile along the line of sight, while around overdense regions infall compresses it. The intermediate quartiles Q2 and Q3 have weak cross-correlations of opposite signs, close to the mean field, and auto-correlations 5--6 times below those of the extreme quartiles.

The ASTRA signals are systematically weaker, by a factor $\sim1.6$ at the peak of the Q1 and Q4 cross-correlations ($s^2\xi_0\simeq-90$ and $+90\,h^{-2}{\rm Mpc}^2$, against $-145$ and $+145$ for density split). This is consistent with the partial mixing of the quartiles: ASTRA relies on $\sim15$ neighbours per point, which dilutes the contrast between environments. For density split the Q1 and Q4 auto-correlations are nearly equal, whereas for ASTRA the Q1 auto-correlation peaks 40\% above the Q4 one: ASTRA's underdense quartile is relatively more clustered with itself, a first sign that its auto-correlations carry distinct information. The relevant question is not the amplitude of the signal but its information content relative to its noise, which we address with the forecasts.

\subsection{Real space and redshift space}

To isolate the effect of RSD, we also measured the fiducial catalogue (and the eight catalogues of the derivative pairs) in real space, the environments being then defined from the real-space positions (r-split) instead of the redshift-space ones (z-split), along the three lines of sight. Figure~\ref{fig:realz} compares the two. RSD increase the monopoles at $20$--$60\Mpch$ by a factor 1.6 for the galaxy 2PCF and by 1.3--1.5 for the Q1 and Q4 cross-correlations of both methods, and create quadrupoles that are absent in real space: positive around underdense regions (outflows) and negative around overdense ones (infall). In absolute value the quadrupoles of density split are 1.6--1.7 times larger than those of ASTRA split, following their larger monopoles; relative to the monopole, however, the ASTRA quadrupole is the larger one ($|\xi_2|/|\xi_0|\simeq0.8$ for ASTRA vs $0.6$ for density split for Q1, $1.0$ vs $0.8$ for Q4, at $20$--$60\Mpch$): the ASTRA environments are less contrasted but their redshift-space anisotropy is relatively stronger. Real-space environments are not observable, so all the forecasts use z-split; the real-space measurements are kept as a diagnostic. (A first version of this work used real space as a ``truth'' to quantify ``systematic biases'' of redshift-space forecasts; since both measurements and emulator are in redshift space, this comparison does not describe a bias of the analysis, and the relevant systematic, the emulator error, is included in the covariance.)

\section{Covariance, derivatives and Fisher forecasts}
\label{sec:framework}
% =====================================================================

\paragraph{Data vectors.} By default, the cross-correlations of the extreme quartiles Q1 and Q4 with the galaxies, $\ell=0,2$, $20\le s\le150\Mpch$ in $10\Mpch$ bins (13 bins per multipole, 52 numbers per method), with or without the 2PCF (26 numbers). Variants add the quartile auto-correlations, use the galaxy quartiles, single quartiles, or change the minimum scale.

\subsection{Covariance}

\paragraph{Baseline: 1786 independent boxes.} The covariance is measured on the 1786 official small boxes (Section~\ref{sec:official}): 1786 independent $(500\Mpch)^3$ volumes treated exactly like our subvolumes. Forecasts are quoted for this volume of $0.125\,(h^{-1}{\rm Gpc})^3$. The correlation matrix (Figure~\ref{fig:corr}) shows strong correlations between neighbouring scales, between the Q4 cross-correlations and the 2PCF, and anti-correlations between the Q1 and Q4 blocks. The ASTRA and density-split blocks of the same quartile are very strongly correlated: being measured on the same galaxies, their noise is largely common. This is the origin of their complementarity: when two measurements share most of their noise but respond differently to the parameters, their combination cancels part of that noise and gains much more than independent measurements would.

\paragraph{First approach: 192 subvolumes of one box.} Before the official mocks were used, the covariance was estimated from the fiducial catalogue of the emulator suite (\texttt{c000}, \texttt{hod239}, the c000 draw closest to the centre of the HOD prior), using the 64 subvolumes measured along the three lines of sight, $N_s=192$ samples. The three lines of sight are not independent, an approximation commonly used in simulation-based analyses. The inverse covariance is corrected by the Hartlap factor \cite{Hartlap2007}, $(N_s-N_d-2)/(N_s-1)$: 0.72 for one method ($N_d=52$), 0.59 with the 2PCF ($N_d=78$), 0.31 for ASTRA + DS + 2PCF ($N_d=130$), which motivated the compression of the data vectors (Section~\ref{sec:fishermat}). Section~\ref{sec:choices} shows how replacing this covariance by the independent one changed the comparison.

\subsection{Finite differences: the HOD-mismatch problem}
\label{sec:finitediff}

The AbacusSummit derivative grid provides, for each parameter, two simulations differing only by a small change of that parameter and sharing the same initial conditions. A natural estimate of the derivatives is the paired finite difference: for each parameter we select one HOD file in each cosmology of the pair, minimising the distance between the two HOD parameter vectors (Table~\ref{tab:hods}), and difference the statistics subvolume by subvolume and line of sight by line of sight before averaging, so that the cosmic variance common to the two simulations largely cancels. This cancellation is only partial: each HOD catalogue is an independent draw of the galaxies populating the halos, so the galaxy sampling noise is not shared, and the difference of two full-box measurements with different HODs has an error comparable to that of a single box (1.05 times, median over the pairs of the grid). The precision of the paired derivatives comes from the average over the 192 paired samples.

Averaged over the 192 samples, these differences have small statistical errors, but they are not cosmological derivatives: because the HODs of the two cosmologies differ, each difference is dominated by the response to the HOD mismatch, 10--15 times larger than the response to the cosmological parameter. The derivative vectors with respect to $\omega_b$, $n_s$ and $\sigma_8$ are almost collinear (correlations 0.95--0.99 in the Fisher metric) and the Fisher matrices are not positive definite. We tried to calibrate and subtract the HOD response with 72 additional fiducial catalogues spanning the HOD parameters of the pairs: a linear model predicts left-out catalogues with a 45\% error, a Gaussian-process model (one GP per principal component of the data vector, anisotropic kernel) with 8\%. Since the HOD term is an order of magnitude larger than the cosmological one, even 8\% leaves a residual comparable to the signal, and the corrected Fisher matrices remain unstable.

The finite differences are nevertheless not discarded. They (i) diagnose and quantify the limitation of an emulator suite for derivatives, a pitfall generic to suites in which the HOD is sampled independently in each cosmology; (ii) once corrected for the HOD response, provide a check of the emulator derivatives that does not rely on a neural network (Figure~\ref{fig:emu_pairs}); (iii) with several HOD draws per cosmology, become an independent estimator of the derivatives by regression over all the pairs (Section~\ref{sec:regression}).

\subsection{Neural-network emulator}
\label{sec:emulator}

\paragraph{Why an emulator.} As in SUNBIRD \cite{CuestaLazaro2024}, we model each statistic as a smooth function of the cosmological and HOD parameters, and take its derivatives at exactly fixed HOD by automatic differentiation, which removes the HOD mismatch by construction.

\paragraph{Inputs and outputs.} The inputs are the 8 cosmological parameters varied in AbacusSummit ($\omega_b$, $\omega_{\rm cdm}$, $\sigma_8$, $n_s$, $\alpha_s$, $N_{\rm ur}$, $w_0$, $w_a$; $h$ and $A_s$ follow from $\theta_*$ and $\sigma_8$) and the 12 HOD parameters, rescaled to $[0,1]$ over the prior. The output of one network is one statistic, the monopole and quadrupole for $20\le s\le150\Mpch$ in $10\Mpch$ bins (26 numbers), measured on the full box as the mean of the 64 subvolumes, standardised by its mean and by the statistical error of the full box $\sigma_{\rm box}$. There is one network per statistic (33 statistics).

\paragraph{Architecture and training.} Fully-connected networks with three hidden layers of 256 units and SiLU activations, smooth so that their derivatives are well behaved, trained with Adam and early stopping on a validation set. With the standardisation above the loss is a $\chi^2$ per bin. Each statistic has an ensemble of 10 networks with different initialisations; the prediction is the ensemble mean, and the scatter between members measures the uncertainty due to training.

\paragraph{Training set.} 999 catalogues of the 85 cosmologies: 8 HOD draws per cosmology, the 72 fiducial calibration catalogues, and 16 draws close to the fiducial HOD in c000 and in each of the 14 cosmologies of the derivative grid, where the derivatives are evaluated (densification, below). Validation and test sets are 8 cosmologies each, never seen in training.

\paragraph{Accuracy.} On the test cosmologies the emulator reproduces all 33 statistics with a median error of 0.4--0.9 $\sigma_{\rm box}$, about ten times below the error of the $(500\Mpch)^3$ volume of the forecasts (Figure~\ref{fig:emu}); the emulator error is nevertheless added to the covariance. A learning curve, retraining on 25, 50, 75 and 100\% of the training set, shows that this error has reached a plateau at $\simeq0.5\sigma_{\rm box}$, set by the galaxy sampling noise of single catalogues: more catalogues would barely help (Appendix~\ref{app:path}).

\paragraph{Cosmological response.} Accurate predictions do not guarantee accurate derivatives. We test the latter by training the emulator without the 14 cosmologies of the derivative grid and comparing its prediction of the $\pm$ pair differences, HOD mismatch included, to the simulations (Figure~\ref{fig:loco}). The response to $\sigma_8$ and $n_s$ is reproduced to 10--20\%, the response to $\omega_b$ to 30--45\% with the correct shape, but the response to $\omega_{\rm cdm}$ is not captured below $s\simeq70\Mpch$: at fixed $\theta_*$ and $\sigma_8$ its effect is smaller than the emulator error and is not learnt away from the training points. This led us to \emph{densify} the training set around the fiducial point with the 16 HOD draws closest to the fiducial HOD in c000 and in each cosmology of the grid (240 catalogues), so that the emulator sees the response to each parameter for many similar galaxy--halo connections. Densification alone did not stabilise the derivatives: the Fisher errors of the individual networks scattered by 70--170\% for the 2PCF and for $\omega_{\rm cdm}$ and $n_s$, and the ASTRA/density-split comparison changed sign between trainings. As an independent check, the derivatives of the emulator at fixed HOD agree with the GP-corrected pair differences to 1--3\% for $n_s$ and $\omega_{\rm cdm}$, 5--6\% for $\sigma_8$ and 8--13\% for $\omega_b$ (Figure~\ref{fig:emu_pairs}).

\paragraph{Training on catalogue differences.} To constrain the response near the fiducial point directly, we add to the loss the $\chi^2$ of the predicted \emph{differences} between catalogues, over 2544 pairs: all the pairs $(c_+,h_i),(c_-,h_j)$ of the seven $\pm$ pairs of the grid with HODs within one prior range of the fiducial, and pairs of the same cosmology (HOD response); details in Appendix~\ref{app:maths}. The differences are not much more precise than individual measurements, since the galaxy sampling noise is not shared, but the term constrains their \emph{structure}: the network must reproduce how the statistics change between nearby points of parameter space, which is what the derivatives require. We also tested an additional weight $(25\Mpch/s)^\alpha$, $\alpha=1$, on the small scales. We validated the three variants on 56 local catalogues (4 per grid cosmology) excluded from training, by predicting the differences of all the held-out $\pm$ pairs (Table~\ref{tab:local}, Figure~\ref{fig:local}). The difference term halves the error for $\omega_b$ and $\sigma_8$ and reduces it for all parameters but $\omega_{\rm cdm}$, already reproduced to 11\% near the fiducial; the residuals reach the noise level of the measured differences ($\simeq1\sigma_{\rm box}$), so the data do not allow a more stringent test. The small-scale weight brings a marginal additional gain but makes the forecasts that include the quartile auto-correlations unstable, so we use the difference term without it: this is the baseline emulator.

\subsection{Derivatives from a regression over pairs}
\label{sec:regression}

As an alternative to the emulator, we estimate the derivatives locally from the derivative grid alone. For a pair of cosmologies $c_\pm$, all the catalogues of each cosmology within a distance $r_{\max}$ of the fiducial HOD $h_0$ (the 16 draws of the densification, the pair draw and the emulator draws) are fitted with a first-order expansion in the HOD parameters, $\boldsymbol d_c(h) = \boldsymbol a_c + G\,(h-h_0)$, with a response $G$ shared by the two cosmologies; the derivative is $(\boldsymbol a_{c_+}-\boldsymbol a_{c_-})/(\theta_+-\theta_-)$, the difference between the two cosmologies at exactly the fiducial HOD. The fit is performed subvolume by subvolume, so that part of the cosmic variance cancels, and the errors are obtained by bootstrapping the subvolumes. The intercepts are interpolated in the middle of draws surrounding $h_0$, and curvature common to the two cosmologies cancels in the difference. This estimator relies on a linear model and on the derivative grid only, while the emulator is a non-linear model of the whole suite: agreement between the two is a strong test of the derivatives.

\paragraph{Reduced HOD space.} With 12 HOD parameters and $\sim18$ catalogues per cosmology, the fit is poorly conditioned: its leave-one-out error is 0.8--1.0 times the scatter between HODs, and separate responses $G_\pm$ or quadratic terms do not help. We therefore restrict it to the most important directions of the HOD space, determined independently from the 72 fiducial calibration catalogues: the right singular vectors of the linear HOD response, each weighted by its statistical error. The singular values are $1, 0.054, 0.014, 0.013,\dots$: \emph{the clustering statistics respond essentially to a single combination of the 12 HOD parameters}, plausibly the large-scale galaxy bias. Keeping 3 or 4 directions, the leave-one-out error drops to 0.7--0.9 times the HOD scatter, at the level of the measurement noise, and the two choices give the same forecasts within 5\%. We use 3 directions.

\subsection{Fisher matrices}
\label{sec:fishermat}

The derivatives $D=\partial\boldsymbol d/\partial(\boldsymbol\theta,\boldsymbol h)$ are obtained at the fiducial point for the 4 cosmological parameters $\boldsymbol\theta=(\omega_b,\omega_{\rm cdm},n_s,\sigma_8)$ and the 12 HOD parameters $\boldsymbol h$. The covariance is the sum of the measured covariance and of the (diagonal) emulator error. The Fisher matrix $F = D^T\hat C^{-1}D + P_h$ includes a Gaussian prior $P_h$ on the HOD parameters of width 0.29 times the prior range (the standard deviation of the uniform prior); the cosmological errors are marginalised over the HOD by inverting the full $16\times16$ matrix, and we also quote errors at fixed HOD. Marginalising over the HOD degrades the constraints by less than $\sim30\%$, the most affected parameter being $n_s$. The uncertainty due to the emulator training is estimated from the scatter of the forecasts of the individual networks, divided by $\sqrt{10}$.

\paragraph{Compression.} Each data vector is compressed to the 16 numbers $\boldsymbol t=W^T\boldsymbol d$, $W=\tilde C^{-1}D$ ($\tilde C$: Ledoit--Wolf shrinkage estimate of the covariance), whose $16\times16$ covariance is then estimated from the samples (Hartlap factor 0.91 with 192 samples). The compression is lossless if $\tilde C=C$ and otherwise loses information without biasing the forecast; it allows long and combined data vectors to be compared fairly.

\paragraph{Results with the single-box covariance.} With the 192-subvolume covariance and the \texttt{hod239} fiducial, the baseline emulator and the regression over pairs, which rely on different assumptions, agreed that ASTRA split was as constraining as or slightly more constraining than density split for the random quartiles (Table~\ref{tab:ratio}): by 0--20\% on $\omega_b$, $n_s$ and $\sigma_8$, the $\sigma_8$ ratio being above unity for both estimators. \subsection{DESI-like mocks: ASTRA split, density split and the 2PCF}

\paragraph{Individual quartiles.} Figures~\ref{fig:q_astra} and~\ref{fig:q_ds} show, as in the density-split analyses, the constraints of each quartile alone and of the four together, and Figures~\ref{fig:c_astra} and~\ref{fig:c_ds} compare the 2PCF, the void-like (Q1) and cluster-like (Q4) quartiles, all quartiles, and all quartiles with the 2PCF. A single quartile constrains the parameters about as well as the 2PCF, the intermediate quartiles (Q3 in particular) being the least constraining, but its information is of a different nature: the 2PCF averages the clustering over all environments, whereas a quartile measures it around one range of densities only, e.g.\ the profile of galaxies around voids for Q1. Each environment responds to the parameters in its own way: the void and cluster quartiles respond with opposite signs and different scale dependences (Figures~\ref{fig:der_astra}, \ref{fig:der_ds}), and their degeneracy directions differ. The gain therefore comes from combining environments: the four quartiles together are several times tighter than any one of them or than the 2PCF, which mixes all environments and loses this distinction. Figure~\ref{fig:quartiles} summarises the information per environment: the Q4 cross-correlations alone already beat the 2PCF on most parameters, Q1 and Q4 together double the gain, and adding the auto-correlations multiplies it again.

\paragraph{Auto-correlations.} Adding the quartile auto-correlations to the cross-correlations tightens the constraints of both methods by factors 1.5--3 (Figure~\ref{fig:autocross}, up to 6--8 times the 2PCF on $\omega_b$); with them, ASTRA split and density split give nearly identical contours.

\paragraph{Scale cuts.} Figure~\ref{fig:smin} shows the errors as a function of the minimum scale. All statistics lose information as small scales are removed, the environment statistics staying 0.2--0.5 dex below the 2PCF up to $s_{\min}=70\Mpch$ (beyond, the 2PCF has fewer bins than parameters). ASTRA and density split stay within 0.1 dex of each other at all scale cuts, their ranking changing with $s_{\min}$, and their combination keeps its 0.2--0.4 dex advantage.

\paragraph{Fiducial point.} Evaluated at four different HODs, the ratio $\sigma_{\rm DS}/\sigma_{\rm ASTRA}$ stays between 0.75 and 1.11 for all parameters (Table~\ref{tab:covbox}): the fiducial point matters at the level of $\pm15\%$.

\subsection{Quijote halos}
\label{sec:quijote}

\paragraph{Covariance.} With 4000 fiducial realisations the covariance is converged: at fixed derivatives the marginalised errors change by less than 1\% between 2000 and 4000 realisations, and by less than 6\% between 1000 and 4000. (About half of the 15\,517 fiducial catalogues of our copy were empty, an incomplete download without consequence given this convergence; all derivative sets are complete.)

\paragraph{Pairing.} The $\pm$ realisations of a given index share their initial conditions: the scatter of the differences between realisations of the same index is 3--4 times smaller than between different indices, for the 2PCF as for the quartile statistics.

\paragraph{Noise of the derivatives.} Even paired, the derivatives remain noisy. Quijote is designed for statistics of the matter and halo fields (power spectrum, bispectrum, \dots). Our observables add two sources of noise that the pairing cannot cancel:
\begin{enumerate}
\item \emph{Sparse tracers.} With $\simeq50\,000$ host halos per $(h^{-1}{\rm Gpc})^3$, each quartile contains only $\simeq12\,000$ halos (or random points), and the shot noise of the quartile statistics is large.
\item \emph{Discrete classification.} A small change of cosmology moves halos and random points from one quartile to another. This re-classification is only partly common to the $+$ and $-$ simulations, even with identical initial conditions, and adds a noise that does not cancel in the difference.
\end{enumerate}
This noise is mostly amplified by the six-parameter Fisher matrix. $\Omega_b$, $h$ and $n_s$ (and $M_\nu$) are strongly degenerate in the clustering of halos: their weakest combination is constrained by a very small amount of information, of the same order as the noise left in the average of 500 derivative pairs, so that along this direction the derivatives are dominated by noise. The standard Fisher estimator is then biased high, and subtracting the expected noise term makes the six-parameter matrices non positive definite (Appendix~\ref{app:maths}).

\paragraph{Estimator and reliability criterion.} We therefore use the approach of \cite{CoultonWandelt2023}: the standard estimator is biased high by the derivative noise, a compressed estimator (half of the simulations define the compression, the other half measure its information) is biased low; the two bracket the true Fisher information, and their geometric mean is nearly unbiased. Where they agree, the forecast does not depend on the derivative noise: we call a forecast \emph{reliable} when the compressed and standard errors differ by less than a factor 1.5 for both statistics compared.

\paragraph{Choice of the parameters.} The bias grows with the length of the data vector and is concentrated in the degenerate directions. We computed the forecasts for six parameter subsets, from $(\Omega_m,\sigma_8)$ to all six parameters, and three compressions of the data vector ($10\Mpch$ bins with $\ell=0,2$; $20\Mpch$ bins with $\ell=0,2$; $20\Mpch$ bins, monopole only; Figure~\ref{fig:qsubsets}). Only $(\Omega_m,\sigma_8)$ and $(\Omega_m,n_s,\sigma_8)$ give reliable forecasts, for all compressions; as soon as $\Omega_b$, $h$ or $M_\nu$ are varied, the two estimators differ by factors 2--10 and the ranking of the methods changes from one compression to the next. These parameters cannot be constrained with these observables and 500 derivative pairs. Fixing $\Omega_b$, $h$ (and $n_s$), as many density-split forecasts do, is justified by external constraints (BBN, CMB); $M_\nu$ would require denser tracers or many more derivative simulations.

\paragraph{Results.} The reliable forecasts (Table~\ref{tab:quijote}) agree with the DESI-like analysis for the random-quartile cross-correlations: density split is as constraining as ASTRA split or more ($\sigma_{\rm DS}/\sigma_{\rm ASTRA}=0.6$--$1.1$). When the quartile auto-correlations are added, or with the halo quartiles, ASTRA split is more constraining on $\sigma_8$ by a factor 1.5--2 and comparable on $\Omega_m$ and $n_s$: its information lies more in the clustering of its environments with themselves and in the environments of the tracers than in the tracer profiles around random environments.

\subsection{Inference with the emulator (MCMC)}
\label{sec:mcmc}

Fisher forecasts assume Gaussian posteriors and are local. To test the full pipeline as it would be applied to data, we sampled the posterior of the 20 parameters (8 cosmological, 12 HOD) with the emulator as the model: Gaussian likelihood with the official covariance rescaled to the data volume plus the emulator error, uniform priors over the training range, \texttt{emcee} with 64 walkers and 8000 steps (Appendix~\ref{app:maths}). The data are measurements the emulator has never seen: one subvolume of each of 2 catalogues of the 8 test cosmologies, and 8 subvolumes of the official base box of the fiducial cosmology, each analysed with the 2PCF, ASTRA split, density split and their combinations (120 chains).

The posteriors are unbiased and calibrated or slightly conservative: the truth lies within $1\sigma$ of the posterior mean in 70--100\% of the runs depending on the parameter (68\% expected). They are however barely narrower than the priors (Figures~\ref{fig:mcmc} and~\ref{fig:mcmc_off}): one $(500\Mpch)^3$ volume with 12 free HOD parameters constrains only $\omega_{\rm cdm}$ and $\sigma_8$ beyond the prior, by 10--15\%, and the statistics differ by a few per cent. A first set of runs on the full $(2\,h^{-1}{\rm Gpc})^3$ box was limited the other way: the emulator error (0.4--0.9 times the statistical error of that volume) was as large as the statistical error, and masked the differences between the statistics. Distinguishing the statistics through inference therefore requires both a large volume and an emulator error well below the statistical error of that volume; with the present training set this is not the case (and the learning curve shows that more catalogues of the same kind would not change it), so the comparison of the methods rests on the Fisher forecasts. The inference pipeline is validated and ready for a larger or less noisy training set.

\section{Robustness tests}
\label{sec:robustness}
% =====================================================================

We test the sensitivity of the measurements to the numerical choices on the first 8 subvolumes of the fiducial catalogue (line of sight $z$). Each test is repeated for both methods, and the change of the Q1 and Q4 cross-correlation monopoles is expressed in units of the statistical error of one subvolume (Figure~\ref{fig:robustness}).

\paragraph{Periodic padding.} Increasing the padding around each subvolume from 30 to $60\Mpch$ leaves the ASTRA values of $r$, and therefore all the statistics, unchanged: the Delaunay neighbourhood of every point of the subvolume is already contained in the $30\Mpch$ padding.

\paragraph{Number of randoms.} Doubling the number of random points in the tessellation ($N_r=2N_g$) shifts the ASTRA Q1 cross-correlations by up to $4\sigma$ and the Q4 ones by up to $2\sigma$ at $s\lesssim40\Mpch$, while density split, for which the randoms are only query points, changes by less than $0.2\sigma$. In ASTRA the randoms enter the definition of $r$ itself (Eq.~\ref{eq:astra_r}), so $N_r/N_g$ is a parameter of the statistic and must be kept fixed.

\paragraph{Sampling density.} Randomly thinning the galaxies (and the randoms in the same proportion) by a factor 2 (5) shifts the ASTRA cross-correlations by 7--10 (20--35)$\sigma$ at $s\simeq20$--$40\Mpch$, against $\sim1$ (3--5)$\sigma$ for density split. At the level of individual points, the rank correlation between the $r$ of the same galaxies computed in the full and in the thinned samples is only $0.65$, $0.38$ and $0.24$ for thinning factors of 2, 5 and 10, and only 58\%, 42\% and 36\% of the galaxies of the extreme quartiles keep their quartile.

This is an intrinsic property of the estimator rather than a numerical instability. ASTRA is an \emph{adaptive-scale} statistic: the environment of a point is defined by its Delaunay neighbours, so its effective scale is the typical distance between neighbours, $\propto\bar n^{-1/3}$, and it changes with the sampling density. Thinning enlarges this scale, which is why the changes are concentrated at $s\lesssim50\Mpch$, whereas density split keeps a fixed smoothing radius. This is not a limitation for the present analysis or for DESI, since the density is fixed in the mocks as in the data (and in an emulator trained at that density). It implies however that ASTRA statistics must be compared, modelled and emulated at the same number density and random-to-galaxy ratio, and that comparisons with density split must be made at equal density, as throughout this paper.

\section{Discussion}
\label{sec:discussion}
% =====================================================================

\paragraph{What is robust.} The comparison was made with three derivative estimators (emulator, regression over pairs, Quijote finite differences), two covariances, four fiducial HODs, two tracer populations (LRG-like galaxies, halos) and several data vectors. Two conclusions never changed: ASTRA split and density split carry comparable information (error ratio 0.75--1.2 for the same data vector in the DESI-like analysis with the independent covariance, 0.6--2 in the reliable Quijote forecasts), and they are complementary, their combination gaining 25\% to a factor 2.4. The ranking for individual parameters changed with the analysis choices (Table~\ref{tab:evolution}), at the 10--40\% level, which is the size of the effect of those choices; we therefore do not claim a winner. Where a trend is consistent, density split is slightly ahead on $\omega_b$, $\Omega_m$ and $n_s$ with the random-quartile cross-correlations, and ASTRA split ahead on $\sigma_8$ through its environment auto-correlations and tracer environments (Quijote). In particular, the advantage of ASTRA split on $\sigma_8$ found with the single-box covariance disappears with the independent covariance.

\paragraph{What we learnt about the methodology.} (i) Emulator suites in which the HOD is drawn independently in each cosmology do not allow naive finite differences: the HOD mismatch dominates the cosmological response by an order of magnitude, and paired differences do not cancel the galaxy sampling noise of different HOD catalogues. (ii) Single-box covariances, even with many subvolumes and lines of sight, can bias the \emph{comparison} of statistics, not only their absolute errors. (iii) Environment statistics of sparse tracers have noisy derivatives, which a six-parameter Fisher matrix amplifies along its degenerate directions; comparing two Fisher estimators that bracket the truth identifies the reliable forecasts. (iv) The clustering statistics respond essentially to a single combination of the 12 HOD parameters.

\paragraph{Limitations.} (i) The forecasts are for one $(500\Mpch)^3$ volume, where the errors are comparable to the prior widths: they compare information content and are not realistic constraints. (ii) The inference is calibrated but not yet discriminating: at this volume the prior dominates, and for a full box the emulator error does. (iii) The Quijote halos are ten times sparser than the LRGs, and ASTRA's adaptive scale is correspondingly larger. (iv) The emulator suite has a single HOD realisation per point, whose galaxy sampling noise sets the floor of the emulator error.

\paragraph{Improvements.} A learning curve shows that the emulator error has reached a plateau at about half the statistical error of a $2\,h^{-1}$Gpc box: more training catalogues of the same kind would barely help. Several HOD realisations (seeds) per point, or HOD-matched catalogues generated with AbacusHOD in all cosmologies, are needed both to lower this floor, which would make an inference on a DESI-like volume discriminating, and to obtain clean derivatives in the DESI-like setting. Larger sets of independent boxes would remove the remaining dependence on the covariance.

\paragraph{Beyond quartiles.} Being local and free of smoothing, ASTRA provides more than a density ranking: a classification of every galaxy into voids, sheets, filaments and knots, a continuous environment value usable as a mark, and a natural treatment of survey masks through the random points. Its quartile auto-correlations already appear to break degeneracies (Quijote); exploiting these other outputs is left to future work.

% =====================================================================
\section{Conclusions}
% =====================================================================

We compared ASTRA split, an environment estimator built on the Delaunay tessellation of galaxies and random points, with standard density split, on the same DESI-like mocks and Quijote halos, with derivatives from an emulator, a regression and clean finite differences, an independent covariance, and an MCMC inference.
\begin{itemize}
\item The two classifications are strongly but not perfectly correlated ($\rho\simeq0.72$); ASTRA produces weaker environment contrasts, and its environment values hardly respond to cosmology: its information lies in the clustering of its environments.
\item Both improve on the 2PCF by factors 1.7--2.7. They carry comparable information (error ratio 0.75--1.1 in the DESI-like baseline, density split slightly ahead) and are complementary (25\% to a factor 2.4 gain when combined). With Quijote halos, ASTRA is more constraining on $\sigma_8$ by a factor 1.5--2 through its environment auto-correlations and tracer environments.
\item ASTRA is an adaptive-scale statistic, very sensitive to the sampling density and to the random-to-galaxy ratio, and must be modelled at the density of the data.
\item Emulator suites with independent HOD draws per cosmology do not allow naive finite differences, single-box covariances bias the comparison of statistics, and environment statistics of sparse tracers have noisy derivatives; we showed how each was diagnosed and addressed.
\item The emulator inference is unbiased and calibrated; discriminating between statistics with it requires several HOD realisations per training point.
\end{itemize}

\section{Mathematical details}
\label{app:maths}
% =====================================================================

\paragraph{Fisher matrix and Hartlap factor.} For a data vector $\boldsymbol d$ with covariance $C$ estimated from $N_s$ samples, $F_{ij} = \partial_i\boldsymbol d^T \hat C^{-1}\partial_j\boldsymbol d$ with $\hat C^{-1} = \frac{N_s-N_d-2}{N_s-1}C^{-1}$ \cite{Hartlap2007}; the marginalised errors are $\sigma_i=\sqrt{(F^{-1})_{ii}}$ and the errors at fixed other parameters $1/\sqrt{F_{ii}}$. HOD parameters are marginalised by adding a Gaussian prior $P_h$ to the HOD block and inverting the full matrix.

\paragraph{Compression.} $\boldsymbol t = W^T\boldsymbol d$, $W=\tilde C^{-1}D$, with $D$ the derivatives with respect to the 16 parameters and $\tilde C$ a Ledoit--Wolf shrinkage covariance. If $\tilde C=C$, $\boldsymbol t$ keeps all the Fisher information of $\boldsymbol d$; otherwise it keeps less, without bias. The covariance of $\boldsymbol t$ is estimated from the samples, with a Hartlap factor close to unity.

\paragraph{Noise of the derivatives.} Derivatives estimated as an average over $N$ pairs are $\hat D = D + \epsilon$, with a noise of covariance $\Sigma_D/N$. The standard Fisher estimator then has the expectation
\begin{equation}
\langle \hat D^T C^{-1}\hat D\rangle = D^T C^{-1} D + \mathrm{Tr}\big(C^{-1}\Sigma_D\big)/N .
\end{equation}
The second term is a positive bias: the noise adds spurious information. It is roughly the same in all directions of parameter space, but matters only where the true information $D^TC^{-1}D$ is small, i.e.\ in the degenerate directions; the marginalised errors, set by the inverse of the matrix, are controlled precisely by these weak directions. We use three tests:
\begin{enumerate}
\item the noise fraction $\mathrm{Tr}(C^{-1}\Sigma_D)/(N F_{ii})$; above 20--30\% the forecast is suspect;
\item the bracketing of \cite{CoultonWandelt2023}: the standard estimator is biased high; a compressed estimator, in which half of the simulations define the compression and the other half measure its information, is biased low. If both give the same error the noise does not matter; if they differ by factors 2--10 the result reflects the noise, not the physics. We call a forecast reliable when they agree within a factor 1.5, and quote their geometric mean, which is nearly unbiased;
\item convergence: the forecast is recomputed with $N/2$ pairs.
\end{enumerate}

\paragraph{Volume.} The forecasts are computed for one $(500\Mpch)^3$ volume, where the absolute errors are comparable to the prior widths; this is the nature of a comparison of information content and is not corrected. The ratios $\sigma_{\rm DS}/\sigma_{\rm ASTRA}$ do not depend on the volume as long as the covariance scales as $1/V$ and the emulator error is negligible, which holds at this volume.

\paragraph{Emulator loss.} With outputs standardised by the statistical error of a $2\,h^{-1}$Gpc box $\sigma_{\rm box}$,
\begin{equation}
\mathcal L = \Big\langle \frac{(f(x_i) - y_i)^2}{\sigma_{\rm box}^2}\Big\rangle + \lambda \Big\langle \frac{[(f(x_i) - f(x_j)) - (y_i - y_j)]^2}{\sigma_{ij}^2 + \epsilon^2}\Big\rangle,
\end{equation}
where the second average runs over the 2544 pairs of catalogues, $\sigma_{ij}$ is the error of their difference (64 paired subvolumes), $\lambda=1$, $\epsilon=0.05\,\sigma_{\rm box}$.

\paragraph{Regression over pairs.} $\boldsymbol d_c(h) = \boldsymbol a_c + G\,V(h-h_0)$ for $c=c_\pm$, with a shared response $G$ and $V$ the 3 leading directions of the HOD space (singular vectors of the HOD response of the 72 fiducial catalogues); derivative $(\boldsymbol a_+-\boldsymbol a_-)/(\theta_+-\theta_-)$; errors by bootstrap over subvolumes.

\paragraph{Inference.} Gaussian likelihood $-2\ln\mathcal L = (\boldsymbol d - \boldsymbol f(\boldsymbol\theta,\boldsymbol h))^T (C/V_{\rm f} + C_{\rm emu})^{-1}(\boldsymbol d - \boldsymbol f)$, with $C$ the official covariance, $V_{\rm f}$ the data volume in units of $(500\Mpch)^3$ and $C_{\rm emu}$ the emulator error; uniform priors over the training range; \texttt{emcee} with 64 walkers and 8000 steps. Calibration is tested by the fraction of runs whose truth lies within $1\sigma$ of the posterior mean (68\% expected).

\section{Methodological path and intermediate results}
\label{app:path}
% =====================================================================

This appendix details the steps of Table~\ref{tab:evolution} in chronological order.

\paragraph{1. HOD files are not realisations.} A first version of the pipeline averaged the statistics of 64 different HOD files per cosmology to build ``covariance'' and ``derivative'' sets, and measured them in the central $500\Mpch$ cube of each box. The HOD files are different HOD models, not realisations: their scatter ($\sim55\%$) dominated the cosmological differences ($\sim1\%$), and the finite differences were compatible with noise (median $|S/N|<1$ per bin for $\omega_b$, $n_s$, $\sigma_8$). That version also had five implementation problems, all corrected: pair counts assumed periodic boundary conditions in a non-periodic cut box; the 2PCF used the mid-point line of sight from the centre of the box instead of the axis along which the RSD were applied, which erased the quadrupole; quartiles were computed jointly on galaxies and randoms, giving unequal and realisation-dependent sizes; the Delaunay triangulation was computed on the cut box without periodic padding; and ASTRA was first applied to the random catalogue alone, so that the random quartiles carried no information on the galaxy field and their correlations were consistent with zero.

\paragraph{2. HOD-matched pairs.} We then selected, for each $\pm$ pair, the two catalogues with the closest HODs (twice closer than random pairs, Table~\ref{tab:hods}) and differenced them subvolume by subvolume (Section~\ref{sec:finitediff}). The differences are precise but dominated by the residual HOD mismatch: a 2\% change of $\omega_b$ implied tens of per cent changes of the 2PCF at $s\sim20\Mpch$, and the Fisher matrices were singular.

\paragraph{3. HOD calibration.} The HOD response measured on 72 fiducial catalogues and subtracted from the pairs was predicted with a 45\% (linear) and 8\% (GP) error; with an HOD term ten times the signal, even 8\% leaves a residual of the size of the signal.

\paragraph{4. Emulator.} The first emulator, trained on 8 HOD draws per cosmology, reproduced held-out cosmologies to a fraction of the statistical error, but did not capture the response to $\omega_{\rm cdm}$ below $70\Mpch$ (Figure~\ref{fig:loco}). A preliminary comparison with this emulator gave ASTRA and density split comparable overall, density split ahead in the voids (Q1) for $\omega_b$ and $\omega_{\rm cdm}$, and their combination 15--25\% better than either.

\paragraph{5. Densification.} 240 catalogues with HODs close to the fiducial were added in the cosmologies of the derivative grid; this alone did not stabilise the derivatives (70--170\% scatter between networks).

\paragraph{6. Regression over pairs.} Introduced as an emulator-independent estimator (Section~\ref{sec:regression}); it revealed that the statistics respond essentially to one combination of the HOD parameters.

\paragraph{7. Training on catalogue differences.} Halved the error of the predicted differences between held-out catalogues for $\omega_b$ and $\sigma_8$ (21\%$\to$10\%, 13\%$\to$7\%), down to their noise level (Table~\ref{tab:local}); the small-scale weight was dropped because it destabilised the auto-correlation forecasts. With the 192-subvolume covariance, emulator and regression then agreed on ASTRA being as good as or 0--20\% better than density split (Tables~\ref{tab:ratio} and~\ref{tab:sigma_sub}).

\paragraph{Learning curve.} To decide whether more training catalogues would reduce the emulator error, we retrained the emulator of the 2PCF and of the Q1, Q4 random-quartile cross-correlations on 25, 50, 75 and 100\% of the training set. The median test error, in units of $\sigma_{\rm box}$, drops from 0.7--1.3 to 0.5--0.8 between 25 and 50\%, but only to 0.5--0.7 at 100\% (ASTRA Q1: 1.01, 0.59, 0.55, 0.51; density split Q4: 0.69, 0.51, 0.48, 0.46). The error has reached a plateau at $\simeq0.5\sigma_{\rm box}$, comparable to the sampling noise of a single catalogue: doubling the training set ($\simeq230$ node-hours) would improve it by $\sim10\%$ at most, and we did not do it.

\paragraph{8. Covariance.} With the 1786 independent boxes and the same derivatives, ASTRA's lead disappeared (Table~\ref{tab:covbox}).

\paragraph{9. Quijote.} The forecasts were first computed for all six parameters. An automatic convergence test run overnight reported that the covariance was not converged, because it compared full forecasts in which the derivative noise dominates the change; once the two effects were separated, the covariance was found converged and the extra processing it had triggered was cancelled. The six-parameter forecasts were then found to depend on the derivative noise (standard and compressed estimators differing by factors 2--10), which led to the parameter subsets of Section~\ref{sec:quijote}. A first overnight result, ASTRA tighter than density split on $\sigma_8$ by a factor 2--3 and density split tighter on $\Omega_m$, $\Omega_b$, survives in the reliable subsets only for the auto-correlations and halo quartiles.

\paragraph{10. MCMC.} The first inference runs on full boxes were limited by the emulator error; on one subvolume, they are limited by the prior (Section~\ref{sec:mcmc}).
