\section{Covariance, derivatives and Fisher forecasts}
\label{sec:framework}
% =====================================================================

To forecast how well a statistic constrains the cosmological parameters, two ingredients are needed: how much the statistic fluctuates from one realisation of the Universe to another at fixed parameters, its \emph{covariance}, and how much it changes when a parameter changes, its \emph{derivatives}. A statistic is informative if its response to the parameters is large compared with its fluctuations. The Fisher matrix combines the two (Section~\ref{sec:fishermat}). This section explains how we obtained each ingredient, and why each required a dedicated dataset.

\paragraph{Data vectors.} By default, the cross-correlations of the extreme quartiles Q1 and Q4 with the galaxies, $\ell=0,2$, $20\le s\le150\Mpch$ in $10\Mpch$ bins (13 bins per multipole, 52 numbers per method), with or without the 2PCF (26 numbers). Variants add the quartile auto-correlations, use the galaxy quartiles, single quartiles, or change the minimum scale.

\subsection{Covariance}

The covariance sets the error bars of the forecasts and the correlations between the bins and statistics of a data vector. It must be estimated from many independent realisations of the same model, measured exactly like the data. \paragraph{Baseline: 1786 independent boxes.} The covariance is measured on the 1786 official small boxes (Section~\ref{sec:official}): 1786 independent $(500\Mpch)^3$ volumes treated exactly like our subvolumes. Forecasts are quoted for this volume of $0.125\,(h^{-1}{\rm Gpc})^3$. The correlation matrix (Figure~\ref{fig:corr}) shows strong correlations between neighbouring scales, between the Q4 cross-correlations and the 2PCF, and anti-correlations between the Q1 and Q4 blocks. The ASTRA and density-split blocks of the same quartile are very strongly correlated: being measured on the same galaxies, their noise is largely common. This is the origin of their complementarity: when two measurements share most of their noise but respond differently to the parameters, their combination cancels part of that noise and gains much more than independent measurements would.

\paragraph{First approach: 192 subvolumes of one box.} Before the official mocks were used, the covariance was estimated from the fiducial catalogue of the emulator suite (\texttt{c000}, \texttt{hod239}, the c000 draw closest to the centre of the HOD prior), using the 64 subvolumes measured along the three lines of sight, $N_s=192$ samples. The three lines of sight are not independent, an approximation commonly used in simulation-based analyses. The inverse covariance is corrected by the Hartlap factor \cite{Hartlap2007} (Appendix~\ref{app:maths}), $(N_s-N_d-2)/(N_s-1)$: 0.72 for one method ($N_d=52$), 0.59 with the 2PCF ($N_d=78$), 0.31 for ASTRA + DS + 2PCF ($N_d=130$), which motivated the compression of the data vectors (Section~\ref{sec:fishermat}). Section~\ref{sec:choices} shows how replacing this covariance by the independent one changed the comparison.

\subsection{Finite differences: the HOD-mismatch problem}
\label{sec:finitediff}

The AbacusSummit derivative grid provides, for each parameter, two simulations differing only by a small change of that parameter and sharing the same initial conditions. A natural estimate of the derivatives is the paired finite difference: for each parameter we select one HOD file in each cosmology of the pair, minimising the distance between the two HOD parameter vectors (Table~\ref{tab:hods}), and difference the statistics subvolume by subvolume and line of sight by line of sight before averaging, so that the cosmic variance common to the two simulations largely cancels. This cancellation is only partial: each HOD catalogue is an independent draw of the galaxies populating the halos, so the galaxy sampling noise is not shared, and the difference of two full-box measurements with different HODs has an error comparable to that of a single box (1.05 times, median over the pairs of the grid). The precision of the paired derivatives comes from the average over the 192 paired samples.

Averaged over the 192 samples, these differences have small statistical errors, but they are not cosmological derivatives, and this is one of the main findings of this work. A pair difference is the sum of two terms: the cosmological term, the change of the statistics due to the parameter (physics), and the HOD term, the change due to the different galaxy--halo connections of the two catalogues (a change in the galaxy-formation model of the mock, not in cosmology). We estimated the HOD term from 72 additional catalogues of the fiducial cosmology with different HODs, which give the response of the statistics to each HOD parameter, evaluated at the HOD difference of each pair, and compared it, in units of the statistical error, with the cosmological term predicted at fixed HOD: the HOD term is 10--15 times larger. The derivative vectors with respect to $\omega_b$, $n_s$ and $\sigma_8$ are then almost collinear (correlations 0.95--0.99 in the Fisher metric), because they all mostly measure the same HOD response, and the Fisher matrices are not positive definite. We tried to subtract the HOD term: a linear model of the HOD response predicts left-out catalogues with a 45\% error, a Gaussian-process model (one GP per principal component of the data vector, anisotropic kernel) with 8\%. But a correction that is 8\% wrong on a term ten times larger than the signal leaves a residual of $0.08\times10\simeq0.8$ times the signal: the corrected derivatives are still dominated by the error of the correction, and the Fisher matrices remain unstable. Any emulator suite in which the HOD is drawn independently in each cosmology has this problem.

These finite differences remain useful in three ways. First, they measure the problem: they show by how much the HOD term dominates, which tells us that any derivative from such a suite must be taken at fixed HOD. Second, once corrected for the HOD term with the Gaussian process, they give derivatives that do not involve a neural network, against which the emulator can be checked (Figure~\ref{fig:emu_pairs}). Third, with several HOD draws in each cosmology of the derivative grid, they become a regression that estimates the derivatives at exactly the fiducial HOD (Section~\ref{sec:regression}).

\subsection{Neural-network emulator}
\label{sec:emulator}

\paragraph{Why an emulator.} As in SUNBIRD \cite{CuestaLazaro2024}, we model each statistic as a smooth function of the cosmological and HOD parameters, and take its derivatives at exactly fixed HOD by automatic differentiation, which removes the HOD mismatch by construction.

\paragraph{Inputs and outputs.} The inputs are the 8 cosmological parameters varied in AbacusSummit ($\omega_b$, $\omega_{\rm cdm}$, $\sigma_8$, $n_s$, $\alpha_s$, $N_{\rm ur}$, $w_0$, $w_a$; $h$ and $A_s$ follow from $\theta_*$ and $\sigma_8$) and the 12 HOD parameters, rescaled to $[0,1]$ over the prior. The output of one network is one statistic, the monopole and quadrupole for $20\le s\le150\Mpch$ in $10\Mpch$ bins (26 numbers), measured on the full box as the mean of the 64 subvolumes, standardised by its mean and by the statistical error of the full box $\sigma_{\rm box}$. There is one network per statistic (33 statistics).

\paragraph{Architecture and training.} Each network is a fully-connected multilayer perceptron: 20 inputs, three hidden layers of 256 units and 26 outputs, about $1.4\times10^5$ weights. This is the type of network used by SUNBIRD \cite{CuestaLazaro2024} for density-split statistics; the outputs are smooth functions of the inputs, which such networks represent well, and the size is a compromise between flexibility and the $\sim1000$ training catalogues. We did not explore other architectures systematically; the learning curve below indicates that the accuracy is limited by the noise of the training catalogues rather than by the network. We use SiLU activations rather than the common ReLU because they are smooth: the derivatives of a ReLU network are piecewise constant, which would make the Fisher derivatives noisy. The networks are trained with the Adam optimiser, with early stopping when the error on the validation cosmologies stops decreasing. With the standardisation above, the loss is a $\chi^2$ per bin, so that the network is penalised in proportion to the statistical significance of its errors. Each statistic has an ensemble of 10 networks with different initialisations; the prediction is the ensemble mean, and the scatter between members measures the uncertainty due to training.

\paragraph{Training set.} 999 catalogues of the 85 cosmologies: 8 HOD draws per cosmology, the 72 fiducial calibration catalogues, and 16 draws close to the fiducial HOD in c000 and in each of the 14 cosmologies of the derivative grid, where the derivatives are evaluated (densification, below). Validation and test sets are 8 cosmologies each, never seen in training.

\paragraph{Accuracy.} On the test cosmologies the emulator reproduces all 33 statistics with a median error of 0.4--0.9 $\sigma_{\rm box}$, the statistical error of a full $2\,h^{-1}$Gpc box (Figure~\ref{fig:emu}). The errors are largest at the smallest scales and in the monopoles. What this means depends on the volume analysed: it is about ten times below the error of the $(500\Mpch)^3$ volume of the forecasts, so for our comparisons the emulator error is negligible (it is nevertheless added to the covariance); for a full $(2\,h^{-1}{\rm Gpc})^3$ box it is comparable to the statistical error, so an analysis of such a volume, or of a DESI-like volume, would be limited by the emulator. A learning curve, retraining on 25, 50, 75 and 100\% of the training set, shows that this error has reached a plateau at $\simeq0.5\sigma_{\rm box}$, set by the galaxy sampling noise of single catalogues: more catalogues of the same kind would barely help, and lowering it requires several HOD realisations per training point (Appendix~\ref{app:path}).

\paragraph{Cosmological response.} Accurate predictions do not guarantee accurate derivatives. We test the latter by training the emulator without the 14 cosmologies of the derivative grid and comparing its prediction of the $\pm$ pair differences, HOD mismatch included, to the simulations (Figure~\ref{fig:loco}). The response to $\sigma_8$ and $n_s$ is reproduced to 10--20\%, the response to $\omega_b$ to 30--45\% with the correct shape, but the response to $\omega_{\rm cdm}$ is not captured below $s\simeq70\Mpch$: at fixed $\theta_*$ and $\sigma_8$ its effect is smaller than the emulator error and is not learnt away from the training points. This led us to \emph{densify} the training set around the fiducial point with the 16 HOD draws closest to the fiducial HOD in c000 and in each cosmology of the grid (240 catalogues), so that the emulator sees the response to each parameter for many similar galaxy--halo connections. Densification alone did not stabilise the derivatives: the Fisher errors of the individual networks scattered by 70--170\% for the 2PCF and for $\omega_{\rm cdm}$ and $n_s$, and the ASTRA/density-split comparison changed sign between trainings. As an independent check, the derivatives of the emulator at fixed HOD agree with the GP-corrected pair differences to 1--3\% for $n_s$ and $\omega_{\rm cdm}$, 5--6\% for $\sigma_8$ and 8--13\% for $\omega_b$ (Figure~\ref{fig:emu_pairs}).

\paragraph{Training on catalogue differences.} To constrain the response near the fiducial point directly, we add to the loss the $\chi^2$ of the predicted \emph{differences} between catalogues, over 2544 pairs: all the pairs $(c_+,h_i),(c_-,h_j)$ of the seven $\pm$ pairs of the grid with HODs within one prior range of the fiducial, and pairs of the same cosmology (HOD response); details in Appendix~\ref{app:maths}. The differences are not much more precise than individual measurements, since the galaxy sampling noise is not shared, but the term constrains their \emph{structure}: the network must reproduce how the statistics change between nearby points of parameter space, which is what the derivatives require. We also tested an additional weight $(25\Mpch/s)^\alpha$, $\alpha=1$, on the small scales. We validated the three variants on 56 local catalogues (4 per grid cosmology) excluded from training, by predicting the differences of all the held-out $\pm$ pairs (Table~\ref{tab:local}, Figure~\ref{fig:local}). The difference term halves the error for $\omega_b$ and $\sigma_8$ and reduces it for all parameters but $\omega_{\rm cdm}$, already reproduced to 11\% near the fiducial; the residuals reach the noise level of the measured differences ($\simeq1\sigma_{\rm box}$), so the data do not allow a more stringent test. The small-scale weight brings a marginal additional gain but makes the forecasts that include the quartile auto-correlations unstable, so we use the difference term without it: this is the baseline emulator.

\paragraph{Derivatives.} Figure~\ref{fig:derivs} shows the baseline derivatives of the 2PCF and of the Q1, Q4 cross-correlations, as the change of each statistic for a small change of each parameter, in units of the statistical error. Three features matter for the forecasts. (i) The two methods respond very similarly to $\omega_{\rm cdm}$ and $n_s$, mostly through the monopole at $s<75\Mpch$: these parameters change the shape of the clustering as a function of scale. (ii) The Q1 (void) cross-correlations respond with the opposite sign to Q4 and to the 2PCF. A single statistic cannot tell a change of cosmology from, e.g., a change of galaxy bias, which also rescales the amplitude; two statistics that respond in opposite directions to the parameter but in the same direction to the bias can, which is how environments break degeneracies. (iii) At fixed HOD, $\sigma_8$ acts mostly on the quadrupoles, i.e.\ through the velocity field. Figures~\ref{fig:der_astra} and~\ref{fig:der_ds} show the response of the four quartiles: it is ordered from Q1 to Q4 for $\omega_{\rm cdm}$, $n_s$ and $w_a$, the extreme quartiles responding most, while the monopole responses to $\sigma_8$ and $w_0$ are small and have no simple ordering.

\subsection{Derivatives from a regression over pairs}
\label{sec:regression}

As an alternative to the emulator, we estimate the derivatives locally from the derivative grid alone. For a pair of cosmologies $c_\pm$, all the catalogues of each cosmology within a distance $r_{\max}$ of the fiducial HOD $h_0$ (the 16 draws of the densification, the pair draw and the emulator draws) are fitted with a first-order expansion in the HOD parameters, $\boldsymbol d_c(h) = \boldsymbol a_c + G\,(h-h_0)$, with a response $G$ shared by the two cosmologies; the derivative is $(\boldsymbol a_{c_+}-\boldsymbol a_{c_-})/(\theta_+-\theta_-)$, the difference between the two cosmologies at exactly the fiducial HOD. The fit is performed subvolume by subvolume: the catalogues of the two cosmologies share the same initial conditions, so the same subvolume contains the same structures in both, and their difference is free of most of the cosmic variance. The errors are obtained by bootstrapping the subvolumes (refitting on random resamplings of them), which propagates the scatter between subvolumes into the derivatives. The intercepts are interpolated in the middle of draws surrounding $h_0$, and curvature common to the two cosmologies cancels in the difference. This estimator relies on a linear model and on the derivative grid only, while the emulator is a non-linear model of the whole suite: agreement between the two is a strong test of the derivatives.

\paragraph{Reduced HOD space.} With 12 HOD parameters and $\sim18$ catalogues per cosmology, the fit is poorly conditioned: there are almost as many unknowns as data points, so the fit can reproduce the catalogues it is given but not predict new ones. Its leave-one-out error (each catalogue predicted by a fit to the others) is 0.8--1.0 times the scatter between HODs, i.e.\ the fit predicts barely better than the mean. This is a classical overfitting problem, solved by reducing the number of free directions. We determine them independently, from the 72 fiducial calibration catalogues: a singular value decomposition of the linear HOD response of the statistics (each weighted by its statistical error) gives the combinations of HOD parameters ordered by how much they change the statistics. The singular values are $1, 0.054, 0.014, 0.013,\dots$: \emph{the clustering statistics respond essentially to a single combination of the 12 HOD parameters}, plausibly the large-scale galaxy bias, the others being 20 to 100 times weaker. Keeping 3 or 4 directions, the leave-one-out error drops to 0.7--0.9 times the HOD scatter, at the level of the measurement noise, and the two choices give the same forecasts within 5\%. We use 3 directions.

\subsection{Fisher matrices}
\label{sec:fishermat}

The derivatives $D=\partial\boldsymbol d/\partial(\boldsymbol\theta,\boldsymbol h)$ are obtained at the fiducial point for the 6 cosmological parameters $\boldsymbol\theta=(\omega_b,\omega_{\rm cdm},n_s,\sigma_8,w_0,w_a)$ and the 12 HOD parameters $\boldsymbol h$. The covariance is the sum of the measured covariance and of the (diagonal) emulator error: the emulator error is an additional uncertainty on the model, independent of the statistical fluctuations of the data, and independent uncertainties add in variance. The Fisher matrix $F = D^T\hat C^{-1}D + P_h$ includes a Gaussian prior $P_h$ on the HOD parameters of width 0.29 times the prior range (the standard deviation of the uniform prior); the cosmological errors are marginalised over the HOD by inverting the full $18\times18$ matrix, and we also quote errors at fixed HOD. Marginalising over the HOD degrades the constraints by less than $\sim30\%$, the most affected parameter being $n_s$: the HOD parameters partly mimic the effect of the cosmological parameters (e.g.\ a change of galaxy bias changes the amplitude of the clustering like $\sigma_8$), and leaving them free absorbs part of the cosmological signal; the small degradation shows that the statistics distinguish cosmology from galaxy formation well. The uncertainty due to the emulator training is estimated from the scatter of the forecasts of the individual networks, divided by $\sqrt{10}$.

\paragraph{Compression.} Each data vector is compressed to one number per parameter (18 numbers), $\boldsymbol t=W^T\boldsymbol d$, $W=\tilde C^{-1}D$ ($\tilde C$: Ledoit--Wolf shrinkage estimate of the covariance), whose covariance is then estimated from the samples (Hartlap factor 0.90 with 192 samples, 0.99 with 1786). The compression is lossless if $\tilde C=C$; otherwise it keeps less information, which makes the forecast errors larger, never smaller, because the Fisher matrix of $\boldsymbol t$ is computed exactly with its own covariance (Appendix~\ref{app:maths}); it allows long and combined data vectors to be compared fairly.
