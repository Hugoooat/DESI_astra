\section{Mathematical details}
\label{app:maths}
% =====================================================================

\paragraph{Fisher matrix.} For a data vector $\boldsymbol d$ (the measured statistics), the Fisher matrix of the parameters $p=(\boldsymbol\theta,\boldsymbol h)$ is
\begin{equation}
F = D^T C^{-1} D + P_h, \qquad D_{ai} = \frac{\partial d_a}{\partial p_i}.
\end{equation}
Each ingredient has a role: $D$ (one column per parameter) says how much each element of the data vector moves when a parameter moves; $C^{-1}$ weights these movements by the inverse of the noise, so that a change counts in proportion to its significance, with the correlations between bins taken into account; $P_h$ adds the prior knowledge on the HOD parameters (here a Gaussian of the width of their uniform prior, on the HOD block only). $F$ is the inverse of the parameter covariance expected for an unbiased, optimal analysis: the marginalised errors are $\sigma_i=\sqrt{(F^{-1})_{ii}}$ (all other parameters free) and the errors at fixed other parameters $1/\sqrt{F_{ii}}$. Comparing two statistics amounts to comparing their $\sigma_i$.

\paragraph{Hartlap factor.} The covariance is estimated from $N_s$ simulated samples. The inverse of an estimated covariance is biased: with noise, $\hat C$ has some accidentally small eigenvalues, so $\hat C^{-1}$ is on average too large, and the Fisher errors too small. For Gaussian data the bias is known exactly, and it is removed by the factor \cite{Hartlap2007}
\begin{equation}
\hat C^{-1}_{\rm unbiased} = \frac{N_s-N_d-2}{N_s-1}\,\hat C^{-1},
\end{equation}
where $N_d$ is the length of the data vector. The factor approaches 1 when $N_s\gg N_d$, and decreases quickly when $N_d$ approaches $N_s$: with 192 samples it is 0.72 for $N_d=52$ but 0.31 for $N_d=130$, which also increases the noise of the forecast. This is why we compress long data vectors, and why 1786 independent boxes (factor 0.99 after compression) are much better than 192 subvolumes.

\paragraph{Compression.} $\boldsymbol t = W^T\boldsymbol d$, $W=\tilde C^{-1}D$, with $D$ the derivatives with respect to the 18 parameters and $\tilde C$ a Ledoit--Wolf shrinkage covariance: $\boldsymbol t$ has one element per parameter, each a weighted sum of the data designed to be as sensitive as possible to that parameter. If $\tilde C=C$, $\boldsymbol t$ keeps all the Fisher information of $\boldsymbol d$. If $\tilde C$ is only an approximation, the weights are not optimal and $\boldsymbol t$ keeps less information. This cannot bias the forecast towards optimism: $\boldsymbol t$ is a linear function of the data, its derivatives are $W^TD$ and its covariance $W^TCW$ is measured from the samples, so its Fisher matrix $(W^TD)^T(W^TCW)^{-1}(W^TD)$ is exact for $\boldsymbol t$, and it is always smaller than or equal to that of $\boldsymbol d$. A poor compression can only make the errors larger. The covariance of $\boldsymbol t$ is short (18 elements), so its Hartlap factor is close to unity.

\paragraph{Noise of the derivatives.} Derivatives estimated as an average over $N$ pairs are $\hat D = D + \epsilon$, with a noise of covariance $\Sigma_D/N$. The standard Fisher estimator then has the expectation
\begin{equation}
\langle \hat D^T C^{-1}\hat D\rangle = D^T C^{-1} D + \mathrm{Tr}\big(C^{-1}\Sigma_D\big)/N .
\end{equation}
The second term is a positive bias: the noise adds spurious information. It is roughly the same in all directions of parameter space, but matters only where the true information $D^TC^{-1}D$ is small, i.e.\ in the degenerate directions; the marginalised errors, set by the inverse of the matrix, are controlled precisely by these weak directions. We use three tests:
\begin{enumerate}
\item the noise fraction $\mathrm{Tr}(C^{-1}\Sigma_D)/(N F_{ii})$; above 20--30\% the forecast is suspect;
\item the bracketing of \cite{CoultonWandelt2023}: the standard estimator is biased high; a compressed estimator, in which half of the simulations define the compression and the other half measure its information, is biased low. If both give the same error the noise does not matter; if they differ by factors 2--10 the result reflects the noise, not the physics. We call a forecast reliable when they agree within a factor 1.5, and quote their geometric mean, which is nearly unbiased;
\item convergence: the forecast is recomputed with $N/2$ pairs.
\end{enumerate}

\paragraph{Volume.} The forecasts are computed for one $(500\Mpch)^3$ volume, where the absolute errors are comparable to the prior widths; this is the nature of a comparison of information content and is not corrected. The ratios $\sigma_{\rm DS}/\sigma_{\rm ASTRA}$ do not depend on the volume as long as the covariance scales as $1/V$ and the emulator error is negligible, which holds at this volume.

\paragraph{Emulator loss.} With outputs standardised by the statistical error of a $2\,h^{-1}$Gpc box $\sigma_{\rm box}$,
\begin{multline}
\mathcal L = \Big\langle \frac{(f(x_i) - y_i)^2}{\sigma_{\rm box}^2}\Big\rangle \\
+ \lambda \Big\langle \frac{[(f(x_i) - f(x_j)) - (y_i - y_j)]^2}{\sigma_{ij}^2 + \epsilon^2}\Big\rangle,
\end{multline}
where the second average runs over the 2544 pairs of catalogues, $\sigma_{ij}$ is the error of their difference (64 paired subvolumes), $\lambda=1$, $\epsilon=0.05\,\sigma_{\rm box}$.

\paragraph{Regression over pairs.} $\boldsymbol d_c(h) = \boldsymbol a_c + G\,V(h-h_0)$ for $c=c_\pm$, with a shared response $G$ and $V$ the 3 leading directions of the HOD space (singular vectors of the HOD response of the 72 fiducial catalogues); derivative $(\boldsymbol a_+-\boldsymbol a_-)/(\theta_+-\theta_-)$; errors by bootstrap over subvolumes.

\paragraph{Inference.} Gaussian likelihood $-2\ln\mathcal L = (\boldsymbol d - \boldsymbol f(\boldsymbol\theta,\boldsymbol h))^T (C/V_{\rm f} + C_{\rm emu})^{-1}(\boldsymbol d - \boldsymbol f)$, with $C$ the official covariance, $V_{\rm f}$ the data volume in units of $(500\Mpch)^3$ and $C_{\rm emu}$ the emulator error; uniform priors over the training range; \texttt{emcee} with 64 walkers and 8000 steps. Calibration is tested by the fraction of runs whose truth lies within $1\sigma$ of the posterior mean (68\% expected).
